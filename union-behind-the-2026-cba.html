THE UNION BEHIND THE DEAL

Nneka Ogwumike became the public face of a historic CBA. The work behind it belonged to an entire union.

Home›Herstory›The Union Behind the Deal

The headlines told us the 2026 collective bargaining agreement was historic. The signed 409-page agreement shows exactly how. It raised the economic ceiling, but it also rebuilt the floor beneath the roster: minimum salaries, team spending requirements, retirement money, pregnancy protections, childcare, family planning, developmental jobs and formal ways for players to remain involved in league operations after bargaining ended. That is why this is not simply a story about stars getting paid. It is a labor story about the whole locker room.

Nneka was the captain. The union was the team.

Nneka Ogwumike had already spent a decade as WNBPA president by the time the new agreement took effect. She helped lead the union through the 2020 CBA, the pandemic era, rapid commercial growth, expansion and a new level of attention around women’s basketball. By the time these negotiations arrived, players were bargaining inside a business that looked dramatically different from the one they had negotiated around five years earlier.

Ogwumike’s role was enormous. She spoke publicly for the union, participated in negotiations, helped hold together a membership with very different career stages and economic interests, and carried strike authority after players overwhelmingly authorized the executive committee to use it if bargaining required it. But the final agreement itself reinforces the central idea behind her leadership: collective bargaining is bigger than any one player.

The 2026 CBA is part of Nneka Ogwumike’s basketball legacy, but calling it “Nneka’s deal” would miss the lesson. The power was collective, and many of the most important gains are designed for players who will never sign the richest contract on the roster.

The seven players with bargaining authority

During the 2025 and 2026 negotiations, the seven player leaders on the WNBPA executive committee were Ogwumike, Kelsey Plum, Napheesa Collier, Breanna Stewart, Alysha Clark, Elizabeth Williams and Brianna Turner. Their union titles mattered because each role carried responsibility beyond being a recognizable player.

The lineup changed after the bargaining period, so these titles are presented as part of the negotiation history. Union leadership is an office players hold for a period of time. The agreement belongs to the membership that authorized and ratified it.

The less visible bargaining table

The executive committee was only the top layer. Reporting during the talks described a typical player negotiating committee of at least 33 players, the seven executive committee members plus 26 team player representatives, with additional players able to participate. At the major 2025 All-Star bargaining session, more than 40 players attended.

That matters because a reserve guard, a veteran forward, a player on a minimum contract, a mother, a rookie, an All-Star and a player fighting to keep a roster spot can experience the same CBA very differently. Team representatives connect the bargaining table to those locker-room realities. The final vote remained with the membership.

Solidarity did not mean silence

The negotiation was not a perfectly smooth march. Players debated strategy, revenue definitions, proposals and the pace of talks. That is not evidence of a weak union. It is evidence of a democratic one. Strong labor coalitions argue internally because different members carry different risks, then decide what they can stand behind together.

In December 2025, 93 percent of players participated in a strike authorization vote and 98 percent voted yes. The vote did not automatically call a strike. It gave the executive committee authority to call one if bargaining required it. Once the new CBA became effective, Article XXXIX established the negotiated no-strike and no-lockout rules for the agreement’s term.

What the signed agreement actually changed

The final CBA lets us move beyond headlines and read the machinery underneath them. The 2026 team salary cap is exactly $7 million. The minimum team salary is 85 percent of that cap, meaning a team that finishes below $5.95 million in Team Salary owes the shortfall to its players under a distribution method determined under the agreement.

PLAYER MINIMUMS$270K to $300K in 2026

The minimum rises with years of service: $270,000 for zero years, $277,500 for one to three, $285,000 for four to six, $292,500 for seven to nine and $300,000 for ten or more.

MAXIMUMS$1.19M standard · $1.4M supermax

The standard maximum equals 17% of the cap. The supermax equals 20% and is reserved for the qualifying contract situations spelled out in Article V.

ROOKIE SCALENo. 1 pick starts at $500K

The signed 2026 rookie scale lists $500,000 for the first pick’s first season, with exact amounts for each first-round slot and later rounds.

TEAM SPENDING FLOOR85% minimum team salary

This is one of the less glamorous but crucial protections. Teams cannot simply benefit from a higher cap while choosing not to spend a meaningful share of it.

Revenue sharing is a formula, not a slogan

The agreement defines Shared Basketball Revenue as the prior year’s League Revenue plus Team Revenue. The salary cap after 2026 is then connected to a defined share of those revenue buckets, after accounting for specified benefits and adjustments.

For the 2027 Salary Cap Year, the agreement’s designated share uses 30.25 percent of 2026 League Revenue plus 12 percent of 2026 Team Revenue. The league-revenue share becomes 30.5 percent beginning with the next calculation, while the team-revenue share rises over the agreement from 12 percent to 13 percent. The cap itself is protected from extreme annual swings: 2027 cannot rise or fall by more than 13 percent from 2026, and later annual changes are generally limited to 10 percent.

There is another protection hiding in the math. If the revenue formula would have produced a larger cap but the annual growth limit holds the cap down, the excess can create a Salary Cap Limit Shortfall. If aggregate player salaries finish below the aggregate salary-cap amount, that can create a Guarantee Shortfall. The CBA combines applicable shortfalls into a League Shortfall that must be paid to players who were on WNBA rosters during that regular season. In other words, the cap limiter is not simply a way to keep money away from the players.

Why the role players matter

A $1.4 million supermax makes the headline. A spending floor, minimum salary schedule, benefits after a waiver, retirement contributions, family protections and temporary replacement rules decide whether the agreement improves the career of the eighth, tenth, twelfth or developmental player too. The signed document makes that middle of the roster impossible to treat as an afterthought.

The benefits tell the role-player story

Some of the clearest evidence of what the union secured appears outside the salary tables. These provisions are not reserved for MVP candidates. They affect players building ordinary professional lives inside an unusually competitive labor market.

401(K)140% employer match

The plan adds a 140 percent employer match on eligible player deferrals. It also includes regular employer contributions equal to 2% of Base Salary at two years of playing service, 3% at three years and 4% at four or more years for eligible players.

VETERAN RECOGNITION$30K · $50K · $100K

Eligible current and retired veterans receive one-time recognition payments based on pre-agreement service. Retired players who previously won WNBA MVP receive the $100,000 tier regardless of service length.

RETIRED PLAYER HEALTHUp to $1,250 annually

Beginning in 2027, qualifying retired players with four or more years of service can receive a health reimbursement benefit for eligible preventative-care expenses, subject to the CBA’s coverage and employment conditions.

PREGNANCY100% salary protection

A player who cannot render services because of pregnancy receives 100% of the applicable Base Salary she otherwise would have received, or the weekly stipend for a Developmental Player, for the period defined in Article X.

CHILDCAREUp to $1,250 per month in 2026

The CBA provides qualifying childcare reimbursement and requires teams to accommodate eligible children and caregivers on team travel under the agreement’s rules.

FAMILY PLANNING$20K per year · $60K career max

Eligible players, spouses or domestic partners can receive reimbursement for specified adoption, surrogacy, egg-freezing and fertility or infertility costs not otherwise covered.

NON-BIRTHING PARENT LEAVETwo paid weeks

Standard Players who qualify as non-birthing parents receive two weeks of paid parental leave, which do not have to be taken consecutively.

LIFE + CAREER PROGRAMS$700K life insurance

The CBA maintains a $700,000 group life policy for non-developmental players and requires at least $250,000 per calendar year for qualifying education, training and career programs.

Developmental players finally have a defined lane

The two developmental spots per team are more specific than a generic “practice player” label. A Player Development Contract carries a base stipend calculated at $750 per week of the regular season, prorated when necessary, plus an Active Games Bonus for games in which the player is activated.

A team may carry no more than two Developmental Players. An individual developmental player can be activated for the greater of 12 regular-season games or 25 percent of the scheduled season, rounded to the nearest whole number. The contract cannot exceed one season and does not carry Base Salary protection, but it contains a team option to convert the player to a Regular Contract at the applicable minimum if the team has cap room or an exception. Developmental salaries are excluded from Team Salary.

That structure does not erase roster insecurity, but it creates an actual employment category, compensation rules and a path to conversion where the league previously had less formal room for development.

Family, health and facilities are working conditions

The agreement goes beyond checks. By the start of the 2027 season, teams must provide a safe family room at the home arena. Nursing mothers must have private accommodations that are not bathrooms, plus access to refrigeration for breastmilk. Practice-facility standards become enforceable beginning with the 2028 season, with reasonable-efforts expectations beginning in 2027.

These provisions explain why collective bargaining cannot be reduced to one salary figure. A professional workplace includes where players train, how they recover, what happens when they become parents and whether their families can function around the demands of the job.

Player voice did not end when bargaining did

Article XXXVI creates a Player Advisory Panel to discuss league operations, improve player-league relations and foster the WNBA’s growth and success. The panel includes five players, the WNBPA Executive Director or designee, the Commissioner or designee and up to three team representatives. It must meet face to face at least once each offseason.

That may sound less dramatic than a salary number, but it matters. The CBA does not treat player voice as something that exists only when a contract is expiring. It creates a formal place for that voice inside the life of the agreement.

One legal detail worth remembering

The CBA is effective as of March 19, 2026 and, absent use of the negotiated early-out provision, continues through January 15, 2033. Either the WNBA or WNBPA may give notice by January 15, 2031 to terminate it effective January 15, 2032. The agreement also explicitly says it is separate from the NBA/NBPA CBA and should be interpreted on its own terms. That is why Front Office 101 should explain WNBA rules as WNBA rules rather than assuming an NBA comparison controls the answer.

Herstory is also labor history

WNBA history is usually organized around championships, MVPs, records and iconic shots. Labor belongs in that archive too. Every rookie who enters on a larger scale, every veteran who receives recognition money, every parent using a negotiated benefit, every retired player accessing preventative-care support and every developmental player working inside a defined contract category is living inside a structure other players negotiated.

That makes Ogwumike’s presidency one of the defining off-court careers in league history. It also makes Plum, Collier, Stewart, Clark, Williams, Turner, the team representatives, participating players, union staff and the broader membership part of the same chapter. Some were superstars. Some were classic role players. Some did their most important work in rooms fans never saw.

The biggest lesson from the signed CBA is not that the stars won. It is that the union wrote a deal whose value can be found all the way down the roster.
