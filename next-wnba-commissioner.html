Cathy Engelbert is retiring at the end of 2026, and the WNBA is not the league she inherited in 2019.

That sentence is both the strongest argument for her legacy and the clearest warning for whoever follows her.

The WNBA announced Engelbert's retirement on September 4, bringing an end to more than seven years under the league's first commissioner. Before Engelbert, the WNBA's top executive held the title of president. She arrived from Deloitte with a business résumé, a growth plan, and the difficult assignment of convincing the sports world to stop treating the WNBA like a promising side project.

She leaves behind a league with 15 teams, plans to reach 18 by 2030, charter air travel, larger audiences, soaring franchise values, and a new collective bargaining agreement capable of producing the first million dollar salaries in league history.

THE LEGACY IN ONE LINEEngelbert helped move the WNBA into a different neighborhood. The next commissioner must make sure the players feel at home there.

What Engelbert built

Her first full season arrived during a global pandemic. The 2020 Wubble kept the league playing while giving its athletes a national platform for racial justice and civic engagement.

The Commissioner's Cup followed in 2021. In 2022, Engelbert led a $75 million capital raise intended to support marketing, digital development, international growth, and league operations.

Charter flights eventually became standard. The Finals expanded. The regular season grew to 44 games and will increase to 50 in 2027. Golden State, Toronto, and Portland expanded the league's footprint, with Cleveland, Detroit, and Philadelphia scheduled to bring the WNBA to 18 teams by 2030.

The new seven year collective bargaining agreement may become Engelbert's most important closing achievement. The agreement established a $7 million salary cap for 2026, projected an average salary of approximately $583,000, and created a path toward contracts worth more than $1 million per season. It also codified charter travel, improved facilities and benefits, and expanded support for families and retirees.

The part of the legacy that cannot be placed on a revenue chart

Engelbert's relationship with players became the central weakness of her leadership.

Players repeatedly criticized the league for reacting too slowly to concerns involving officiating, online abuse, personal safety, and accountability. Napheesa Collier's public criticism in 2025 turned private frustration into a league wide conversation about whether WNBA leadership truly listened to its players.

Engelbert later acknowledged that the league needed to do better if players did not feel appreciated or heard. The new CBA proved that the league and union could still complete historic work together, but it did not magically repair every relationship.

Both parts of Engelbert's record deserve space. She helped create the strongest business foundation the WNBA has ever had. She also showed that a commissioner cannot lead this particular league through financial strategy alone.

The next commissioner's assignment

The WNBA's next commissioner must speak two languages fluently. She must understand the economics of owners, media companies, and corporate partners while respecting the experiences of the athletes whose talent, personalities, and advocacy created the product being sold.

The next commissioner does not need to be the players' best friend. Commissioners work for ownership. She does, however, need enough trust to disagree with players without making them feel dismissed.

That is the assignment. Here is the early scouting board.

EARLY BOARD · SEPTEMBER 2026 Seven candidates, seven different versions of the job

These names are potential candidates, not confirmed finalists. The search may include executives who have not yet been publicly identified.

LEAD CANDIDATEA

Swin Cash

President, Women's Sports Foundation · Hall of Famer · Former WNBA and NBA executive

Cash offers the most complete bridge between the court, front office, and ownership suite. She played 15 seasons in the WNBA, won three championships, and later gained NBA basketball operations experience with the New Orleans Pelicans. In 2026, she became president of the Women's Sports Foundation.

Cash was also among the investors in the WNBA's 2022 capital raise. Any ownership interest would need to be reviewed and properly addressed before she became commissioner.

Best trait
Credibility across players, executives, sponsors, and fans.
The question
Has she managed an organization with the financial, legal, and operational complexity of an entire league?
Verdict
The strongest all around profile on the early board.
THE SCALE CANDIDATEA

Sarah Mensah

President, Jordan Brand · Former Portland Trail Blazers chief operating officer

Mensah may be the strongest pure business operator in the group. She leads Jordan Brand's global strategy, marketplace, and business portfolio. Before joining Nike, she spent 19 years with the Portland Trail Blazers and eventually oversaw revenue strategy, marketing, communications, digital operations, community impact, and facility operations.

Best trait
Basketball business scale and global brand leadership.
The question
Can she quickly build authentic player relationships and master labor and basketball operations?
Verdict
If ownership wants an executive who can scale the business, Mensah belongs near the top.
THE CONTINUITY CANDIDATEA minus

Bethany Donaphin

Head of WNBA League Operations · Former WNBA player

Donaphin knows how the league actually works. Since becoming head of league operations in 2018, she has overseen on court operations, rules, scheduling, scouting, conduct standards, and venue compliance. She would enter the job understanding the personnel, systems, and daily pressures that an outside candidate would need months to learn.

Best trait
Immediate command of the league's daily machinery.
The question
Can an established league insider become the face of meaningful change?
Verdict
The safest operational choice, although not necessarily the cleanest cultural reset.
THE EXPANSION BUILDERA minus

Jess Smith

President, Golden State Valkyries · Former Angel City FC revenue executive

Smith has already built something the rest of the league wants to copy. Golden State's launch turned the Valkyries into a ticketing, sponsorship, and community success. Her work earned the WNBA's inaugural Business Executive Leadership Award.

Best trait
Modern expansion instincts and proof that women's sports can become an event and a community.
The question
Is leading one WNBA franchise enough preparation to govern an entire league and answer to every ownership group?
Verdict
The fastest riser on the board, and future commissioner material even if this search comes early.
THE PLAYER TRUST CANDIDATEB plus

Nneka Ogwumike

WNBPA president · Retiring as a player after the 2026 season

No candidate would arrive with more immediate credibility among players. Ogwumike has served as WNBPA president since 2016 and helped represent players through two collective bargaining agreements. She understands compensation, benefits, working conditions, league politics, and the personal consequences of executive decisions.

Best trait
Player trust, policy fluency, and a decade of labor leadership.
The question
Can the league's leading player advocate become the owners' chief executive without weakening the trust that makes her candidacy attractive?
Verdict
A powerful possibility, although an empowered player strategy role may be the better first step.
THE GOVERNANCE WILDCARDB plus

Condoleezza Rice

Stanford professor · Former United States secretary of state · WNBA equity investor

Rice's WNBA connection is much deeper than her title in a candidate graphic suggests. She became an equity partner in the league's 2022 capital raise. At Stanford, she served as Chiney Ogwumike's faculty mentor, advising her as she balanced basketball, academics, and plans beyond the court.

Rice also chaired the NCAA's Commission on College Basketball, which examined relationships among schools, athletes, agents, apparel companies, and outside basketball organizations. That gives her significant experience in governance, negotiation, crisis management, and institutional reform.

Best trait
Governance, diplomacy, institutional strategy, and the ability to command difficult rooms.
The question
Does the WNBA need a high level diplomat, or a full time sports operator with deeper league experience?
Verdict
A legitimate candidate, not ceremonial casting. She becomes especially intriguing with a strong basketball operations executive beside her.
THE REVENUE SPECIALISTB plus

Renie Anderson

NFL executive vice president and chief revenue officer

Anderson knows how the biggest sports league in the country turns attention into durable revenue. She oversees partnerships, sponsorships, consumer products, new business, and media sales.

Best trait
Major league commercial expertise and a deep sponsor network.
The question
Can she combine revenue skill with the player relationships and cultural fluency this league urgently needs?
Verdict
The business sleeper, especially if she arrives prepared to listen before rebuilding the machine.

The We Know the W pick

Swin Cash is the best fit today.

She has lived the WNBA from nearly every angle: player, champion, executive, entrepreneur, advocate, nonprofit leader, and investor. She offers the clearest opportunity to preserve Engelbert's business momentum while repairing the emotional distance between the league office and its players.

Sarah Mensah may be the strongest outside executive. Bethany Donaphin would provide the smoothest operational transition. Jess Smith has already demonstrated how quickly a women's sports property can become an event, a community, and a thriving business. Condoleezza Rice is the most fascinating wildcard because her relationship with the WNBA, Stanford athletes, and sports governance is real.

The ultimate answer may not be one perfect résumé. The WNBA may need a commissioner with Swin Cash's basketball credibility, Sarah Mensah's business scale, Jess Smith's expansion instincts, Nneka Ogwumike's player trust, and Condoleezza Rice's ability to navigate powerful institutions.

Unfortunately, creating that candidate in a laboratory would probably violate the new CBA.

Engelbert leaves the next commissioner a stronger league. She also leaves a harder job. Expectations are higher, the money is larger, the scrutiny is louder, and players are no longer willing to accept being treated as supporting characters in the story their labor created.

The next commissioner must understand that the WNBA does not need to be rescued.

It needs to be respected, protected, and led into the future it has already earned.
