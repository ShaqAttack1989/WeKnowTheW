THE UNION BEHIND THE DEAL

Nneka Ogwumike became the public face of a historic CBA. The work behind it belonged to an entire union.

Home›Herstory›The Union Behind the Deal

When the WNBA’s new collective bargaining agreement was completed in 2026, the biggest numbers were impossible to miss. A $7 million salary cap. A $1.4 million maximum salary. A comprehensive revenue sharing model. More than $1 billion in projected salaries and benefits over seven years. But the agreement was not simply a financial story. It was a labor story, and one of the most important chapters in WNBA Herstory belongs to the players who did the bargaining work while still doing their basketball jobs.

Nneka was the captain. The union was the team.

Nneka Ogwumike had already spent a decade as WNBPA president by the time the 2026 agreement was signed. She had helped lead the union through the 2020 CBA, the pandemic era, the league’s rapid commercial growth, new media rights, expansion and the surge of attention that transformed the value of WNBA basketball. By 2025, the players were not negotiating from the same market reality they had occupied five years earlier.

Ogwumike’s role was enormous. She spoke publicly for the union, sat through negotiations, helped hold together a membership with different career stages and economic interests, and carried strike authority after the players overwhelmingly authorized the executive committee to use it if necessary. She also kept returning to the same principle: if the business grew, the players who created the product needed to grow with it.

The 2026 CBA is part of Nneka Ogwumike’s basketball legacy, but calling it “Nneka’s deal” would miss the point. Collective bargaining works because the power is collective.

The seven players with bargaining authority

During the 2025 and 2026 negotiations, the seven player leaders on the WNBPA executive committee were Ogwumike, Kelsey Plum, Napheesa Collier, Breanna Stewart, Alysha Clark, Elizabeth Williams and Brianna Turner. Their union titles mattered because each role carried responsibility beyond being a recognizable player.

The lineup changed after the bargaining period, so these titles are presented as part of the negotiation history. That distinction matters. Union leadership is an office players hold for a period of time, while the CBA belongs to the membership that authorized and ratified it.

The less visible bargaining table

The executive committee was only the top layer. The WNBPA describes its negotiating structure as a committee made up of executive leaders and other players representing the full spectrum of the league. Reporting during the talks put the typical player CBA committee at at least 33 players: the seven executive committee members plus 26 team player representatives, with additional players able to join.

That is the part of labor history that often disappears. A reserve guard, a veteran forward, a player on a minimum contract, a mother, a rookie, an All-Star and a player trying to keep a roster spot can experience the same CBA very differently. A good union contract has to make room for all of those lives. The team representatives were the connective tissue between the negotiating room and locker rooms across the league.

At the 2025 All-Star bargaining session, more than 40 players attended. The union also used conference calls, written updates and open bargaining participation to keep the broader membership connected. WNBPA rules make the final authority clear: all players vote on whether to accept the CBA.

Solidarity did not mean silence

The negotiation was not a perfectly smooth march. Players debated strategy, proposals, revenue definitions and the pace of talks. That is not evidence of a weak union. It is evidence of a democratic one. Strong labor coalitions argue internally because different members have different risks, then decide what they can stand behind together.

In December 2025, 93 percent of players participated in a strike authorization vote and 98 percent voted yes. The vote did not automatically call a strike. It gave the seven-player executive committee the authority to call one if bargaining required it. That leverage mattered when talks extended into 2026 and the previous CBA entered a status quo period.

The long nights before the big numbers

OCT. 2024
Players opt out

The WNBPA exercised its option to end the prior agreement early and begin bargaining for a new economic framework.

JULY 2025
All-Star solidarity becomes public

More than 40 players attended an in-person bargaining session, while the All-Stars used “Pay Us What You Owe Us” shirts to make the economic dispute visible to fans.

DEC. 2025
Strike authority is approved

With 93 percent player participation, 98 percent voted to authorize the executive committee to call a strike if necessary.

JAN. 2026
Status quo bargaining continues

The deadline passed without another extension. The union kept bargaining without immediately calling a work stoppage.

MARCH 2026
Marathon negotiations

The sides met for consecutive days, including sessions that stretched deep into the night, while revenue sharing and housing remained key issues.

MARCH 20
Tentative agreement

The WNBA and WNBPA announced a seven-year deal with a new revenue sharing system, a $7 million cap and historic salary growth.

MARCH 23–24
Both sides ratify

More than 90 percent of players participated and voted unanimously to approve the agreement. The WNBA Board of Governors unanimously ratified it the next day.

MAY 22
The long-form CBA is signed

The WNBA and WNBPA completed and executed the final written agreement, formally cementing the 2026 through 2032 CBA.

What they changed

The easiest way to understand the stakes is to compare the old economic ceiling with the new one. The 2026 salary cap jumped from roughly $1.5 million to $7 million. The maximum salary rose to $1.4 million. Average salary was projected above $583,000, while minimums reached $270,000 to $300,000 depending on service. The No. 1 draft pick entered a rookie scale projected at $500,000.

The agreement also tied future compensation more directly to league and team revenue through a new revenue sharing model. It expanded retirement support, family planning and benefits for players with children, veteran recognition payments, facility and staffing standards, codified charter travel, performance bonuses, roster size and two developmental player spots. Salary cap exceptions for injured or pregnant players were also part of the new structure.

Why the role players matter

Maximum salaries make headlines. Minimum salaries, roster rules, injury exceptions, retirement contributions, family benefits and job protections determine whether the league works for everyone. A union contract is tested at the bottom and middle of a roster just as much as it is celebrated at the top.

Herstory is also labor history

WNBA history is usually organized around championships, MVPs, records and iconic shots. Labor belongs in that archive too. Every future rookie who sees a larger first contract, every veteran who receives stronger retirement support, every developmental player who gets a formal lane into the league and every player whose salary rises with the business is living inside a structure other players negotiated.

That makes Ogwumike’s presidency one of the defining off-court careers in league history. It also makes Plum, Collier, Stewart, Clark, Williams, Turner, the team representatives, participating players and union staff part of the same chapter. Some were superstars. Some were classic role players. Some did their most important work in rooms fans never saw.

The final vote may be the cleanest summary of what they built. More than 90 percent of the players participated. The answer was unanimous.
