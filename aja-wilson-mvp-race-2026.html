SCORING VOLUME

Who owns the scoreboard?

FOOD FOR THOUGHT

The problem with A’ja Wilson’s MVP case is that dominance can become background noise.

When greatness repeats itself long enough, we start grading it on a curve. A new record feels exciting. A rookie leading the league’s best team feels historic. A generational passer makes every possession look like an argument. Meanwhile, A’ja Wilson is doing the basketball equivalent of holding up the ceiling, and because we have watched her do it before, there is a temptation to treat the ceiling like architecture instead of labor.

Start with the hardest combination in the race.

Wilson is not simply piling up points. She is carrying elite offensive volume while remaining one of the league’s most consequential defenders. The WNBA’s September 17 MVP analysis had her leading the league in scoring, blocks, usage rate and points in the paint. That is an absurd overlap of responsibilities. Most stars bend a game in one direction. Wilson bends both.

That matters because “valuable” is not the same word as “spectacular,” “historic” or “best story.” Those can all be part of an MVP case, but the award asks a bigger question: how much of a team’s identity depends on this player being on the floor?

THE NUMBER I CANNOT GET PAST +26.3

Las Vegas’ net rating swing with Wilson on the court versus off, according to the WNBA’s September 17 MVP analysis.

The Aces do not just get better with her. They become a different team.

With Wilson on the floor, Las Vegas had a 116.6 offensive rating and a +12.1 net rating in the WNBA’s latest MVP check-in. With her off, the offense fell to 94.7 and the net rating cratered to -14.2. That is a 26.3-point net-rating swing. You can debate the noise inside any on/off statistic, but a gap that large is not whispering.

That is the spine of the “hers to lose” argument. Wilson has the counting stats. She has the efficiency. She has the rim protection. She has the team success. Then the impact data says the Aces’ entire ecosystem tilts when she steps across the scorer’s table.

And then Kelsey Mitchell kicked the door in.

Mitchell’s September 18 performance made the race more interesting, not less. She scored 33 against Toronto, pushed her season total to 1,034 points and broke Wilson’s WNBA single-season points record. She has now produced one of the great scoring seasons the league has seen.

But total points and the scoring title are not the same thing. Mitchell reached the record in her 41st game of a 44-game schedule. Wilson’s old mark came in 38 games in 2024, and Wilson still leads the 2026 scoring race by average at 26.1 points per game to Mitchell’s 25.2. The record is a massive piece of Mitchell’s case. It is not, by itself, the whole MVP case.

Olivia Miles owns the cleanest counterargument.

If the ballot starts with “best player on the best team,” Miles has a serious answer. The rookie leads Minnesota in scoring and assists, the Lynx sit atop the standings, and she has done it with veteran-level efficiency and control. The scale of that achievement is easy to understate because she makes running an elite team look normal in Year 1.

The separation comes on defense and individual burden. The WNBA’s own breakdown notes that Minnesota has defended better with Miles off the floor, while Wilson’s value survives the two-way test. Miles can still close that gap with a spectacular final stretch, but right now her case asks voters to emphasize team position and rookie history more heavily than Wilson’s broader individual impact.

The Fever have two MVP cases, which is both a flex and a complication.

Caitlin Clark remains the most devastating creator in this group. Her passing changes geometry, she carries enormous playmaking responsibility, and Indiana is 27–14. Mitchell, however, now owns the historic scoring record and the WNBA’s latest MVP ladder identifies her as Indiana’s most impactful player by on/off net rating. If voters are deciding who has been the Fever’s most valuable player, the existence of two legitimate answers can make it harder for either to overtake a candidate whose team’s case points overwhelmingly to one person.

Paige Bueckers is the “do not blink” candidate.

Bueckers has followed a Rookie of the Year season with another efficient, high-level year while Dallas climbed into the postseason. Her balance matters: scoring, playmaking and shot quality without needing the ball to dominate every second. The limitation is not that her season lacks value. It is that the top of this ballot has produced seasons that demand historical language.

So why is it A’ja’s to lose?

Because every route into the debate eventually runs into something Wilson already does at an elite level. Want scoring? She leads by average. Want efficiency? She is doing it without sacrificing volume. Want defense? She leads the league in blocks and still deters shots that never become box-score events. Want team success? Las Vegas is third. Want direct value? The on/off swing is enormous. Want durability and nightly burden? Her usage sits at the top of the league.

There are better individual arguments against her than there were a month ago. There is not yet a better complete argument.

THE COUNTERCASES

If you are voting for somebody else, this is the argument.

A strong MVP column should be able to make the opposition’s best case before answering it.

OLIVIA MILES

“Best player, best record, historic rookie.”

Minnesota is first at 31–10, Miles leads the Lynx in scoring and assists, and no rookie has carried this exact combination of team success and offensive responsibility since the league’s earliest MVP-level rookie seasons.

What she needs:

A closing week big enough to make team dominance outweigh Wilson’s two-way statistical edge.

KELSEY MITCHELL

“The scoring season became history.”

Mitchell just broke the single-season points record, owns a 25-game streak of 20-point performances and has a +13.3 Indiana net-rating swing in the WNBA’s latest MVP analysis.

What she needs:

The scoring gap by average to narrow, Indiana to keep winning, and voters to treat offensive gravity as decisive despite the defensive gap.

CAITLIN CLARK

“Nobody creates more offense this way.”

Clark’s scoring plus league-shaping passing gives Indiana a second elite offensive engine. Her strongest argument is not just assists; it is the number of defensive decisions she forces before an assist can even happen.

What she needs:

A huge finish and a clear answer to the question of whether she or Mitchell owns Indiana’s 2026 MVP case.

PAIGE BUECKERS

“Efficiency, control and a Dallas climb.”

Bueckers combines 20-plus scoring with nearly six assists and has kept Dallas moving upward without turning every possession into high-usage survival basketball.

What she needs:

A closing burst that forces voters to reconsider the volume gap separating her from the leaders.

FINAL-WEEK WATCH

“Hers to lose” is not the same thing as “race over.”

Three regular-season games remain for several contenders. Here is what would materially change the conversation.

01

Wilson stalls while a challenger detonates.

A quiet finish from A’ja paired with multiple signature games from Miles or Mitchell could change the final impression voters carry into the ballot.

02

Minnesota locks No. 1 with a Miles masterpiece.

The stronger the Lynx finish, the easier it becomes to argue that a rookie running the league’s best team deserves extraordinary weight.

03

Mitchell turns the points record into a scoring-title fight.

The closer Mitchell gets to Wilson in points per game while extending her record, the harder it becomes to separate “historic scorer” from “most valuable.”

04

The Aces slip out of the top tier.

Team record is not everything, but an ugly closing stretch would weaken one of the clean supporting pillars under Wilson’s case.

SHAK’S FOOD FOR THOUGHT

The real MVP test is not “Who gave us the newest story?” It is “Whose absence changes the most basketball?”

Right now, the numbers keep bringing that question back to No. 22 in Las Vegas.
