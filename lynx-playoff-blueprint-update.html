SO, ABOUT THAT
LYNX BLUEPRINT...

Golden State's win in Minnesota was easy to file under “bad night, missing Courtney Williams.” Then the Valkyries kept doing the same thing to everybody else.

By We Know the W Staff5-minute readAugust 28, 2026
Home›Snack Shak›Food for Thought

I wasn't ready to call Golden State's 80–66 win over Minnesota a playoff blueprint after one game. Courtney Williams was out, the Lynx looked out of rhythm, and sometimes a good team just has a lousy Monday. Fair enough. But Golden State has made that explanation a lot less comfortable.

The Valkyries went into New York on Thursday and beat the Liberty 79–60. New York shot 30.2 percent. Veronica Burton handed out 13 assists without turning the ball over once. Golden State also got a real lift from its bench and completed a season sweep of the Liberty.

That matters to Minnesota because the Lynx game no longer looks like an isolated mess. Golden State is doing this on purpose.

The pressure starts before the stars get the ball

The mistake would be treating the Minnesota game as a story about stopping Napheesa Collier. Golden State did more than that. It made Minnesota uncomfortable before the offense ever reached Collier.

The Lynx turned it over 18 times and scored a season-low 66 points. Olivia Miles had eight points and four turnovers. Collier had nine. Minnesota, a team that normally lives comfortably in the paint, finished with only 20 points there.

That's the part I'd circle on the scouting report. Don't wait for Minnesota to run its offense and then try to defend it. Make the Lynx fight just to get into it. Pick up the ball. Crowd the first pass. Get hands into the next one. Make 18 seconds on the shot clock feel like eight.

THE FILM NOTEThe goal isn't simply to guard Minnesota better. It's to make Minnesota start its offense later.

And please don't give the possessions back

There is another side to what Golden State did Thursday that shouldn't get lost in the defense. Burton had 13 assists and zero turnovers.

That's playoff basketball in one line. If you're going to spend all night making an opponent work for possessions, you can't turn around and donate yours. Pressure only becomes a real advantage when the other end is clean enough to cash it in.

This is where the blueprint gets tougher than “play hard defense.” A team has to pressure Minnesota without fouling itself into trouble, rebound well enough to finish the stop, take care of the ball, and still have enough offense left to score. That's why everybody can't copy Golden State just because they watched the tape.

The bench isn't decoration

Golden State's depth keeps showing up in these games, too. Against New York, the Valkyries had an 18-point advantage in bench scoring. Against Minnesota, the Lynx never found enough offense to loosen the game once Golden State's defense settled in.

That feels important for a playoff series. A starting five can win a night. Four games require somebody else to matter. If Minnesota's opponent loses the non-star minutes every time the rotations begin, all that pressure up top eventually becomes a very tiring way to lose.

Now Atlanta gets a turn

Minnesota's next game against Atlanta is the one I want to see before pushing this theory any further.

The Dream don't have to become Golden State. They have their own way of stealing possessions. Angel Reese can do it on the glass. An offensive rebound doesn't show up in the turnover column, but the effect is similar: one team gets another possession and the other team has to defend again.

That's why Atlanta is such an interesting test. If the Dream can make the game physical, create extra possessions and get enough perimeter creation around Reese, they can attack several of the things that have bothered Minnesota without copying the Valkyries possession for possession.

There is still a Courtney Williams-sized asterisk

This part can't be skipped. Williams missed the Golden State loss with a mid-foot sprain and was described as day-to-day. Her absence mattered. She gives Minnesota another ball handler, another midrange option and, maybe most importantly against pressure, another person who can rescue a possession when the first idea goes nowhere.

So no, I'm not declaring the Lynx solved. Not even close.

What I am saying is that the league has more film now. Golden State bothered Minnesota, then went out and smothered another playoff team. That makes the pattern worth watching instead of shrugging off.

Food for thought

If you're trying to beat Minnesota in October, I don't think the recipe starts with “stop Phee.” That's asking for a very long night.

Start earlier. Pressure the ball. Make the first action ugly. Turn a few clean possessions into late-clock possessions. Get into the paint on the other end. Bring a bench that can hold up. Don't throw your own possessions away. And if the game is still there with five minutes left, actually finish it.

Golden State has shown what that can look like. The question now is whether anybody else has the personnel, patience and nerve to do it four times.
