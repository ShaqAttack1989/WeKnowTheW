The best draft class in WNBA history is 2001.

Lauren Jackson and Tamika Catchings supplied the all-time ceiling. Ruth Riley, Deanna Nolan, Katie Douglas and Penny Taylor turned the same class into a championship assembly line. That combination of peak, depth and winning still has no equal.

THE VERDICT

2001 wins because greatness kept showing up after pick No. 1.

Jackson and Catchings became league MVPs and Finals MVPs. Riley and Nolan also won Finals MVP. Douglas and Taylor became All-Stars and champions. Six different careers from one first round could tilt a playoff series.

THE QUESTION TO BECKY HAMMON WAS BUILT ON A BAD NUMBER

The Fever did not have three No. 1 picks.

The reporter grouped Caitlin Clark, Aliyah Boston and Kelsey Mitchell as three consecutive No. 1s. Clark and Boston were first overall. Mitchell was No. 2 in 2018. Indiana’s playoff roster had six top-10 picks and two No. 1s. Las Vegas also had six top-10 picks—but three No. 1s.

The bigger implication:

High picks raise the talent floor; they do not install the system. Vegas converted premium picks into continuity, defensive habits and elimination-game poise. Indiana’s two No. 1s and No. 2 pick accelerated its return to relevance, but the Game 3 loss showed the remaining gap in late-game execution and roster seasoning. Chelsea Gray—picked No. 11—scored 23 in the clincher, the cleanest reminder that development and fit can beat draft slot.

SOURCES + RULES

What this board counts.

Roster rule. “Top-10 pick” means selected No. 1 through No. 10 in the annual WNBA Draft, regardless of which team originally made the pick. Undrafted players and expansion selections do not count. Developmental players appear only if listed on the official roster; none change the top-10 totals.

Founding-year rule. The 1997 Elite Draft and initial player allocations created a different talent pool, so the legacy ranking compares annual collegiate/international drafts and treats 1997 as a structural outlier.
